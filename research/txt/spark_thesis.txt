Abstract
Digital snapshots have become one of the most common ways that people record and
share their experiences in everyday life. Most users would like to take high-quality
photographs with their mobile phones or tablets. However, even with recent advances
in mobile digital cameras, it is still challenging to capture satisfactory images, espe-
cially in low-light situations. In low-light situations, long exposures are inevitable to
improve the image quality, but, in turn, handshake blur becomes signiﬁcant. Accord-
ingly, image deblurring techniques that aim to remove this blur have been intensively
studied. Nevertheless, most image deblurring methods are brittle and computation-
ally expensive, making them hardly usable in practice.
This dissertation presents a new gyro-based multi-image deblurring system that
handles various challenges in real-world situations. Instead of capturing a single long
exposure image, a burst of shorter exposure images is captured along with gyroscope
data being recorded at the same time. Any camera shake that occurred during the
capture is accurately estimated from the gyroscope data and captured images. Finally,
all captured images are jointly deblurred to obtain a sharp and noise-free image of
the scene.
Because both camera motion and the scene are accurately measured with a better
capture method, gyro-based multi-image deblurring is more robust than previous
approaches while its computational burden is signiﬁcantly reduced. To demonstrate
the eﬀectiveness and eﬃciency of the proposed method, image deblurring is applied
to full-resolution imagery of natural scenes captured using a mobile camera and an
attached gyroscope.
In addition to the implementation, this dissertation discusses a key question that
iv

===== PAGE 5 =====
applies to any multi-image deblurring method: How many images should be cap-
tured, and what should their exposure times be? By quantifying the amount of noise
ampliﬁed by deblurring, an analysis is provided to estimate how well multi-image de-
blurring can be done with given exposure parameters. Then, an important property of
multi-image deblurring is presented: Multi-image deconvolution does not beneﬁt from
increasing exposure time beyond a certain threshold. The noise reduction granted by
increasing exposure time is mostly oﬀset by the noise ampliﬁcation incurred by the
more extreme deblurring. Based on this analysis, diﬀerent capture strategies are com-
pared in various photographic situations, demonstrating that multi-image deblurring
is a robust approach that works in low-light situations when other methods fail.

Chapter 1
Introduction
In recent years, digital cameras have become an almost ubiquitous component in mo-
bile devices, making digital photography a part of everyday life. Most users would
like to take high-quality photographs with their mobile phones or tablets. However,
although the performance of digital cameras in mobile devices has signiﬁcantly im-
proved because of recent advances in image sensor technology and software processing,
it is still challenging to capture satisfactory images, especially in low-light situations.
In such low-light situations, handshake blur has signiﬁcant eﬀects on image quality
when hand-held devices are used. Long exposure times are often inevitable to reduce
the amount of image noise, but, in turn, the images may suﬀer from blur due to
handshake occurred during the capture.
Because of this trade-oﬀbetween image
noise and camera shake blur, which is illustrated in Figure 1.1, it is not trivial to
capture an image that is sharp and clean of noise at the same time.
To overcome this trade-oﬀand generate compelling images, this dissertation
presents a gyro-based multi-image deblurring system that combines two techniques:
gyroscope-based camera motion estimation and non-blind multi-image deconvolution.
This system captures a burst of images while the gyroscope data is recorded at the
same time. Then, the camera shake that occurred during the capture is estimated
from both the gyroscope data and image data. Finally, based on the estimated cam-
era motion, all captured images are jointly deconvolved to generate a clean and sharp
image. Although these two techniques have individually been the subject of previous

1.1
Capture Strategies for Removing Handshake
Blur
A capture strategy refers to a way of determining a set of capture parameters, such
as the number of shots in a burst, exposure time and analog gain used for the burst.
This is more general than exposure metering that chooses exposure time and analog
gain for a single image capture. Choosing the number of shots becomes an important
factor when both noise and handshake blur are involved.

===== PAGE 15 =====
CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
3
To reduce the amount of blur caused by camera shake, two approaches are com-
monly used: align-and-average and image deblurring. Although both approaches aim
to solve the same problem, they are based on the two ends of the capture strategy
spectrum. On one end is the capture strategy that uses a short exposure time to
capture multiple images that are assumed to be blur-free but noisy. The align-and-
average method merges these captured images after alignment to reduce noise. On
the other end is the strategy of capturing a clean but blurry image using a long ex-
posure time. From this single image, a sharp image is recovered by image deblurring.
However, there exists a middle ground between these two ends of capture strategies.
A set of images can be captured with intermediate exposure time, hence with some
small amount of blur, and jointly deconvolved to recover the latent image. This ap-
proach is called multi-image deblurring. These approaches are illustrated in Figure
1.2.
Multi-image deblurring has several advantages over other approaches. Exposure
times used in align-and-average must be very short in order to avoid excessive hand-
shake blur, and, as noted above, a short exposure time may result in poor image
quality that makes alignment diﬃcult and may not even be short enough to com-
pletely eliminate hand shake blur. In contrast, multi-image deblurring is applicable
to a wider range of exposure times because the blur can be removed computationally,
and is thus more eﬀective in low-light situations. In addition, multi-image deblurring
is more robust and eﬃcient than single-image deblurring. When multiple images are
captured, each image contains diﬀerent blur caused by diﬀerent camera motion, and
thus preserves frequency components of the scene that are complementary to those
captured in the other images. By combining these images, multi-image deblurring sig-
niﬁcantly improves the quality of output image and reduces ringing artifacts caused
by missing frequency components.

A gyroscope measures the rotational component of motion, while

eliminate the problem of camera motion estimation, and signiﬁcantly improve image
deblurring. As Figure 1.4 illustrates, blind deblurring requires estimating the camera
motion and the scene at the same time. This is an ill-posed problem that consists
of two tightly connected subproblems, each of which is challenging.
Although it
can be solved by an iterative optimization method that alternates between these
two subproblems, blind deblurring is considered brittle and computationally heavy
because the optimization heavily relies on complicated image prior models to obtain
a plausible result. In contrast, if the camera motion is known by the measurements
from the sensors, image deblurring becomes non-blind and more tractable because
non-blind deblurring is solved only once.

1.3
Comparing the Performance of Diﬀerent Cap-
ture Strategies
A key question about any multi-image deblurring method is: for a given total capture
time, how many images should be captured, and what should their exposure times
be? Answering this question requires a method that estimates how well deblurring
performs in a given photographic situation. However, this is a challenging problem
because the performance of image deblurring depends on various factors such as the
scene statistics, the characteristics of applied blur, and exposure parameters used for
the capture.
This dissertation provides a theoretical analysis that answers an important part
of this question. In particular, a method of estimating the performance of multi-
image deconvolution based on a noise ampliﬁcation factor is proposed. This method
relates the performance with exposure parameters, which are intuitive and easily
applicable in practice. Then, this dissertation proves the following property: Multi-
image deconvolution does not beneﬁt from increasing exposure time beyond a certain
threshold. This result goes against the common thought that longer exposure times
improve the image quality. When camera shake blur exists, increasing exposure time
does not help because the noise introduced by computationally removing additional
blur cancels the reduced photon noise in captured images.
Finally, the performance of diﬀerent capture strategies is compared. Our analysis

focuses on comparing multi-image deconvolution and align-and-average, which can
be considered as a special case of multi-image deconvolution when handshake blur is
negligible. Both approaches are compared in various scenarios to show that multi-

to align-and-average. Then, guidelines are formulated to help users to determine how
to capture and process images to get the best output in real-world situations.
1.4
Contributions
The ﬁrst contribution of this dissertation is an implementation of gyro-based multi-
image deblurring system that handles various practical issues. These issues include
gyroscope drift correction, spatially-varying blur that includes oﬀ-center rotational
blur, time synchronization between image data and gyroscope data, electronic rolling
shutter, and handling moving objects and over-exposed regions. All these issues add
complexity to the system, but they must be included in order to produce high-quality
images.
The second contribution is an analysis that allows us to compare the performance
of diﬀerent capture strategies. Based on this analysis, following key points are demon-
strated:
• Multi-image deconvolution does not beneﬁt from increasing exposure time be-
yond a certain threshold.
• Multi-image deconvolution is a good capture strategy in low-light situations
when align-and-average fails.
In the following chapter, a review of other related works on various subproblems
is described. Then, the next two chapters will describe the implementation details of
our gyro-based camera motion estimation (Chapter 3) and image deblurring system
(Chapter 4). Following our analysis of the performance of multi-image deconvolution
(Chapter 5), Chapter 6 will compare the performance of align-and-average and multi-
image deconvolution and formulate guidelines for choosing good strategies. Finally,
Chapter 7 will conclude the dissertation, suggesting some future research directions.

Chapter 2
Related Work
Before we describe our image deblurring system, it is valuable to examine a number
of well-studied problems in computational photography that are closely related to
this system. Section 2.1 reviews the basic building blocks in multi-image deblurring.
Then, Section 2.2 provides an overview on the issues related to using inertial sensors
and compares previous works on image deblurring system based on inertial sensors.
Finally, previous works on comparing capture strategies are presented in Section 2.3.
2.1
Improving Image Deblurring
We focus on two topics that are related to our multi-image deblurring: how camera
shake blur is modeled and how multiple images are used to reduce motion blur.
2.1.1
Modeling Camera Shake Blur
When a camera moves during an exposure, a captured image becomes blurry as the
sensor integrates the relative motion between the camera and the scene. The pose of
the camera continuously changes during an exposure, and each pose gives a slightly
diﬀerent view of the same scene. The diﬀerence between these views causes camera
shake blur.
Many blur models assume a spatially uniform blur [35, 54, 11], which means that
9

===== PAGE 22 =====
CHAPTER 2. RELATED WORK
10
the shape of blur is identical across the entire image. This assumption allows the
blur to be modeled with a well-known 2-D convolution between the scene and a blur
kernel that represents the shape of the blur. The main advantage of this approach is
that the frequency domain analysis is available to characterize the blur. In addition,
the applied blur can be eﬃciently removed in the frequency domain. However, this
uniform blur model is often violated in practice because in-plane rotation is usually
a signiﬁcant contributor to actual camera shake [36]. Thus, this blur model should
be applied only in small local windows that satisfy the uniform blur assumption.
A common approach in modeling camera shake blur is to use geometric represen-
tation of camera motion, which inherently gives spatially-varying blur. Although we
need six degrees of freedom (DOF) to describe full camera motion [30], 3-axis rotation
[60] or in-plane translation and rotation [19] is commonly used as well. The 3-axis
rotation model is a reasonable approximation because the rotational component is
the dominant factor of camera shake. In practice, there are only small changes in
the camera position during capture, and thus, the amount of blur caused by cam-
era translation is negligible for suﬃciently distant scenes. K¨ohler et al. [33] show
that 3-DOF approximation of full camera motion agrees well with real camera shake
measurements.
2.1.2
Multi-Image Approaches to Reducing Motion Blur
In addition to single-image deblurring methods, a number of multi-image approaches
based on diﬀerent data captures have been proposed.
One category of approaches relies on align-and-average methods. In astronomical
photography, lucky imaging methods have been popularly used, dating back to the
1970s [17]. These methods select images that have undergone the least degradation
due to atmospheric turbulence, and merge them after alignment. This idea is ex-
tended in various recent works in computational photography to reduce the amount
of motion blur or to denoise video stream. These methods are predicated on accurate
alignment between images, which can be done based on a global transformation [56],
a global alignment with local adjustments [29], optical ﬂow [39, 49], or gyroscope

11
measurements [51].
Another category is called multi-image deblurring. Papers taking this popular
approach share the observation that combining images with diﬀerent shapes of blur
makes deblurring stable [50, 12, 11]. Li et al. [38] remove blur in video frames to
generate a sharp panorama image.
Agrawal et al.
[5] deliberately vary exposure
times to capture blurry images with diﬀerent shapes of blur for an object moving
at a constant speed. Cho et al. [13] estimate multiple motions within the scene.
However, these methods are based on blind estimation, which is still a challenge. We
use gyroscope measurements to make the estimation non-blind.
Other sets of data are assumed to improve the image quality in low-light situations.
A pair of ﬂash and no-ﬂash images [48], or a pair of noisy and blurry images [63, 57]
can be captured. These methods aim to fuse only the good properties of the two
images. Sharp image details come from ﬂash image or noisy image, while good color
information is transferred from long exposure shot.

Inertial Sensors and Their Applications in Im-

The emergence of MEMS inertial sensors enabled new approaches to solve a number
of well-studied problems in computational photography and computer vision.
By
using the rich data provided by inertial sensors, the complexity of handling image

applications such as video stabilization and rolling shutter correction [31, 20, 26],

and inertial sensor data, and review previous works that tackle these problems. Then,
we focus on image deblurring systems that use inertial sensors and compare them with

gyroscopes are widely adopted in consumer devices, enabling numerous novel applica-

bias, and practical issues must be properly handled to obtain more reliable measure-
ments. The following section will describe how these challenges are tackled.

2.2.2
Challenges in Using Inertial Sensor Measurements
Here, we list a few practical issues that aﬀect the accuracy and reliability of inertial
sensor measurements:
• Unstable bias A bias refers to zero-rate output, which is a non-zero oﬀset that
exists independent of sensor motion. The amount of bias is aﬀected by various
factors, such as temperature, vibrations and mechanical stress [6, 58], and thus
slowly varies with time.
• Unknown time delay between image data and sensor data Timestamps
obtained from diﬀerent sensors in the system suﬀer from unknown time de-
lay caused by data transfer, buﬀering, and software stack delays in operating
systems. This delay varies randomly.
• Electronic rolling shutter In contrast to a global shutter, an electronic rolling
shutter scans across the frame either in vertical or horizontal direction to read
out pixel values. Thus, each row or column is captured at a slightly diﬀerent
time.
• Gravity oﬀset Accelerometers measure proper acceleration that includes grav-
itational acceleration, which must be subtracted to obtain the acceleration due
to the motion.
• Oﬀ-center rotations The measurements from gyroscopes are not aﬀected by
the position of rotation center, and thus cannot distinguish oﬀ-center rotations
with the same angular rate.

drift becomes more critical because the measurements are integrated twice to obtain

2.2.3

The most commercially successful solution for reducing camera shake blur is optical
image stabilization embedded in consumer digital cameras. An optical image stabi-

Recently, optical stabilizers are also adopted in cell phone cameras such as the ones
in Nokia Lumia 920 [1] and HTC One M7 [2]. Although these stabilizers are known

2.3
Finding Good Capture Strategies
Capturing a good set of data is a key task that can improve the performance of
computational photography applications. There exist many previous works that focus
on what capture strategies are best for their applications.
Several papers on high dynamic range (HDR) imaging address this issue of ﬁnding
a set of optimal parameters. Hasinoﬀet al. [22] show that the optimal choice of
exposure time and analog gain signiﬁcantly improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
of the merged HDR result. This improvement comes from using high analog gains
as they are useful for suppressing read noise while reducing the total capture time.
This idea is adopted in a few HDR metering methods that determine scene-speciﬁc
parameters in real time on mobile devices [53, 18].

by Zhang et al. [64]. Their analysis concludes that align-and-average works better
than single-image deconvolution when total capture time is kept the same. Mitra
et al. [43] prove that deblurring a long exposure shot is better than taking a short
exposure shot in low-light situations. However, their blur model is limited to 1-D

3.1.2

We use a geometric model to represent spatially-varying blur, and then adopt a
uniform blur model as a local approximation. The geometric model expresses the blur
as the sum of global transformations that represent a series of camera rotations. We
assume that camera motion is dominated by rotation. This is a common assumption
in image stabilization systems and is valid for suﬃciently distant scenes. The entire
image is then divided into small rectangular blocks that are assumed to contain the
spatially uniform blur. This approximation allows us to apply a theoretical analysis
that provides useful insights into the deblurring process while reducing the complexity
of implementation. Our model assumes that the scene is static and does not directly
handle local motions. In addition, our model does not account for other types of blur,
such as depth-dependent defocus blur and optical blur.

Although gyroscope measurements can be directly applied to evaluate the angular
rotation in Equation 3.8, a drift correction method is applied beforehand whenever
capture takes place to tackle the practical issues described in Section 2.2.2. Our drift
correction method ﬁnds the best camera motion estimation that correlates gyroscope
measurements with captured image frames.
We model the angular rotation by accumulating the incremental changes given as
∆θ(t, t + ∆t) = (ω(t + td) + ωd)∆t
(3.15)
where ω(t) is the rate of rotation measured with gyroscope, td is the time delay, ωd
is the bias which causes drift, and ∆t denotes the time interval between gyroscope
samples. Our method estimates the time delay td, the bias ωd and the rotation center
o = (ox, oy, oz)T. We assume that the rotation center o does not move in z direction,
oz = 0, and the variation in o is negligible during the capture.
Our drift correction method is based on a series of pairwise comparisons and
takes advantage of capturing multiple images. The pairwise comparison, which is
demonstrated in Figure 3.3, quantiﬁes how well the captured images agree with the
estimated camera motion. Similar cost functions are proposed in visual tracking [28]
and image deblurring techniques [50]. Let us assume two capture images, which are
blurry, are denoted as Y1 and Y2. Their blur kernels, represented as K1 and K2,
respectively, are obtained based on gyroscope measurements and current estimation
of ωd, td and o. Then, the two images are blurred again with the other image’s blur
kernel. These two re-blurred images should be the same in the ideal case because the
order of applying blur should not matter, i.e., convolution operator is commutative.
The aggregated error between the two images represents the match score of current
estimation of camera motion.

4.4
Handling Moving Objects and Over-Exposed
Regions
Although our multi-image deconvolution method eﬀectively removes handshake blur
in static regions, moving objects and over-exposed regions are not handled properly.
These regions are considered as outliers because they do not follow our image forma-
tion model described in Equation 3.11. Moving objects may suﬀer from excessive blur
or ghosting artifacts caused by the object motion occurred during the entire capture
time. In addition, pixel values in highlights may be clipped due to limited dynamic
range of image sensor, and often cause artifacts after deconvolution.
In this section, we propose an additional image blending step that follows non-
blind multi-image deconvolution [47]. Our goal is not to handle outliers in a physically
correct manner. Instead, we seek only to hide possible deconvolution artifacts. Figure
4.6 provides an overview of our method. First, outliers are detected in the image
obtained from multi-image deconvolution. Then, the pixel values around outliers are
blended with the result of a patch-based denoising method. As a result, the blended
image contains sharp details in static regions, while moving objects and over-exposed
regions are reconstructed naturally.
The main advantage of using a result from patch-based denoising methods is that
outliers do not cause severe artifacts after denoising. These methods merge similar
image patches to eﬀectively reduce noise in a reference image [10][14]. Because of
the patch search stage, they are inherently robust to moving objects or misalignment
between images.
After the denoising, moving objects remain as blurry as in the
reference image. However, the amount of the motion blur is much less than the case
when multi-image deconvolution is used. Also, over-exposed regions do not cause
artifacts because they are denoised in the same way as other regions.

Chapter 5
Performance of Multi-Image
Deconvolution
In the previous chapters, we discussed the implementation details of our gyro-based
multi-image deblurring system and showed how our system can recover a clean and
sharp image from a set of blurry images and gyroscope measurements. We now move
on to the second part of this dissertation, which aims to answer following questions:
How well will our deblurring be done with given capture parameters? How do we
compare diﬀerent capture strategies and choose the one that will give best results?
The ﬁrst step of answering these questions requires deﬁning how the performance
of multi-image deconvolution can be modeled with given camera shake. In addition,
a method is required to estimate what will be the performance when a certain set of
capture parameters is used. Then, the performance can be compared between diﬀerent
capture strategies. However, estimating the performance of image deblurring is a very
challenging task because two factors are mixed together: camera shake blur and the
scene. Although many image prior models are proposed, it is still hard to model the
characteristics of the scene and estimate their inﬂuence on deconvolution.
In this chapter, we propose a way of modeling the performance of multi-image
deconvolution by using a noise ampliﬁcation factor. The noise ampliﬁcation factor
quantiﬁes the amount of noise that is ampliﬁed by deconvolution. By understanding
the properties of this factor, we can describe the performance in terms of exposure

main tool for this analysis will be the noise ampliﬁcation factor deﬁned in Section 5.2,

6.3

6.5
Guidelines for Choosing Capture Strategies
Here we summarize some guidelines for choosing capture strategies that could be
distilled from the various experiments preformed in the previous sections. Given a
photographic situation, our goal is to determine how many images should be captured,
what their exposure times should be, and which processing, AA or MD, should be
applied.
To begin, let us list some important points that should be considered when AA
or MD is used:
• Align-and-average There exists an upper bound in exposure time that gives
tolerable amount of handshake blur.

===== PAGE 85 =====
CHAPTER 6. ANALYSIS OF ALTERNATIVE CAPTURE STRATEGIES
73
• Multi-image deblurring Exposure time can be increased up to the point at
which read noise is negligible and handshake blur becomes intolerable. Also,
the number of images should be large enough to make deconvolution stable.
The amount of handshake blur is directly related to focal length of the optics.
When a narrow ﬁeld of view is used, the required exposure time to avoid blur may
be too short to capture any meaningful information on the scene. Also, handshake
blur depends on the type of camera and user. These issues should be considered
to determine when AA can give reliable results. In contrast, MD is relatively more
ﬂexible with the choice of exposure time. However, there exists an upper bound in
exposure time that beneﬁts MD. This point comes at shorter exposure times if the
scene is brighter or if more images are captured.
There are more rules that apply to both approaches. First, exposure time should
be long enough to allow accurate camera motion estimation. Because accurate esti-
mation is predicated on having low noise, there exists a lower bound on exposure time.
This lower bound is aﬀected by both scene brightness and scene content. Second, ex-
posure level, which is the combination of exposure time and analog gain, should be
carefully determined to avoid over-exposing bright regions. When a scene with wide
dynamic range is to be captured, the range of proper exposure levels can be limited.
In addition, a few practical issues may limit the choice of capture parameters. For
example, the eﬀective range of analog gain is restricted by the presence of read noise
added before analog ampliﬁcation. Also, limitations in system bandwidth may bound
the choice. For low-bandwidth camera systems, a blanking time may exist between
exposures due to slow readout speed. This blanking time may signiﬁcantly reduce
the eﬃciency of capture because no photon is collected during the time. Further, the
memory buﬀer in the camera system, which is used to store captured images before
they are processed, has a limited size, restricting the maximum value of n. Finally, n
is bounded by the total capture time during which the photographer can expect the
scene to hold still.
In many situations, AA is preferred to MD because the former has lower compu-
tational cost. A good strategy is to ﬁnd a parameter set that gives desired image
quality with AA. However, it may not be possible for certain scenes, for example,

===== PAGE 86 =====
CHAPTER 6. ANALYSIS OF ALTERNATIVE CAPTURE STRATEGIES
74
in low-light environments when read noise is dominant or analog gain has already
been maximized.
In these situations, MD can provide an alternative with better
performance.

Chapter 7
Conclusion
This dissertation presents a new image deblurring system that combines gyro-based
camera motion estimation and non-blind multi-image deconvolution. Our method
tackles various challenges encountered in real-world scenarios to allow practical im-
plementation in mobile systems. Then, a theoretical analysis on multi-image decon-
volution provides insight into capture strategy selection. The key insights can be
summarized:
Multi-image deconvolution is a robust method that is applicable to a wide range
of low-light situations. When this method is used, exposure time can be increased up
to a point when read noise is negligible and handshake blur becomes uncorrelated to
each other.
In this ﬁnal chapter, the limitations of our system are discussed, and we lay out
a few possible directions to extend our system.
7.1
Limitations
The success of our method depends on how faithful our models for image formation
and blur representation can explain the real measurements from image sensor and
gyroscope. In practice, the assumptions we made for the models are often violated
75

===== PAGE 88 =====
CHAPTER 7. CONCLUSION
76
and these violations are the main cause of degradation. Here, we list a few cases.
First, the translational component of camera motion may not be negligible. When
objects are placed close to the camera, or when the user moves the camera around
the scene, gyroscope data cannot provide accurate estimation anymore. During the
gyroscope drift correction stage, the search of proper gyroscope bias is done in a
constrained space that is deﬁned by our rotational model. When translation matters,
we might relax our model and search for image translations as a reﬁnement step.
Although full motion cannot be recovered, this additional step will ﬁnd the best
camera motion that explains the captured images.
Second, obtaining accurate timestamps for each exposure time window is not
trivial. Although the length of exposure time can be considered deterministic in many
camera systems, the blanking time between exposures is not easy to ﬁnd because there
are unexpected time delays caused by the processing pipeline and occasional frame
drops. Frame drops are one of the main causes of poor results in our implementation.
If we allow exposure times to vary during the capture, then reconciling timestamps
becomes more complicated and is speciﬁc to the camera system.
Finally, when a very short exposure time is used with a longer frame time, our
camera motion estimation may suﬀer from large gaps between frames. When long
frame times are used in bandwidth-limited systems, neighboring image frames are
less correlated to each other, and our estimation is more likely to stay in local min-
ima. When images are sharp with tolerable amount of noise, image-based alignment
methods can be a superior alternative.
7.2
Further Applications
Here are a few more possible directions to extend our gyro-based multi-image deblur-
ring method. Our method can be combined with other applications to make it more
robust to tackle real-world situations.

===== PAGE 89 =====
CHAPTER 7. CONCLUSION
77
7.2.1
Removing Blur in Moving Objects
Our method only removes blur in static regions. Although the errors in moving objects
do not cause severe artifacts in the multi-image approach, these objects remain blurry
after deblurring.
Our approach can be combined with other motion deblurring methods [5, 13]
to remove local motion blur that exists in images captured from hand-held devices.
The motion of moving objects can be separated from global blur caused by camera
shake, which is measured by a gyroscope.
As shown in Section 4.4, our camera
motion estimation is robust to the existence of moving objects in the scene because
static regions, which appear to be sharper than moving objects, guide our gyroscope
bias estimation. Then, moving objects can be detected by Equation 4.5 and treated
separately with blind motion deblurring methods. This approach can be extended to
video data as well.
7.2.2
Gyro-Based High Dynamic Range Photography for
Hand-held Devices
In low-light situations, the use of multi-image deblurring methods is often restricted
by their limited capability in handling wide dynamic range scenes. Longer exposure
images can improve output images, but may also blow out interesting image features
that exist in bright regions.
Our gyro-based multi-image deblurring method can be easily extended to tackle
this problem. Instead of capturing a burst with the same exposure parameters, we can
deliberately vary exposure times among captures. A good capture strategy would be
to take an image with short exposure time to preserve highlights and capture multiple
images with long exposures to obtain dark regions, but with some blur. Based on
the camera motion estimated from gyroscope data, these images are jointly deblurred
to recover a sharp HDR image. Our multi-image deblurring, which is described in
Equation 5.1, is not limited to handling images captured with the same exposures.
With a small modiﬁcation to handle over-exposed regions in long exposures, our
method can be applied to an HDR burst.

===== PAGE 90 =====
CHAPTER 7. CONCLUSION
78
This idea is similar to Lu et al. [41]. Our method improves their work by using
gyroscope measurements. Also, it would be interesting to design a new HDR metering
method for hand-held captures. Our analysis on the performance of capture strategies
can help selecting capture parameters that are expected to give good results.
7.3
Closing Remarks
The goal of computational photography is to extend the capabilities of digital pho-
tography.
In particular, this dissertation focuses on obtaining good images with
hand-held devices in low-light situations where a traditional camera fails. In such
situations, an attached gyroscope can be used to measure camera shake and then
computationally recover high-quality images by combining blurry images captured
from a burst capture. We hope that this gyro-based multi-image approach enables
future computational photography applications that run on mobile devices.
In addition, this dissertation analyzes how good capture strategies can be deter-
mined for hand-held image captures.
We hope that key insights provided in this
dissertation inspire camera software designers and researchers in the ﬁeld of compu-
tational photography to tackle various challenges in hand-held digital photography.
