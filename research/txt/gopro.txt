Abstract
The task of image deblurring is a very ill-posed prob-
lem as both the image and the blur are unknown. Moreover,
when pictures are taken in the wild, this task becomes even
more challenging due to the blur varying spatially and the
occlusions between the object. Due to the complexity of the
general image model we propose a novel convolutional net-
work architecture which directly generates the sharp image.
This network is built in three stages, and exploits the ben-
eﬁts of pyramid schemes often used in blind deconvolution.
One of the main difﬁculties in training such a network is to
design a suitable dataset. While useful data can be obtained
by synthetically blurring a collection of images, more real-
istic data must be collected in the wild. To obtain such data
we use a high frame rate video camera and keep one frame
as the sharp image and frame average as the correspond-
ing blurred image. We show that this realistic dataset is key
in achieving state-of-the-art performance and dealing with
occlusions.
1. Introduction
Motion blur artifacts are a major concern while captur-
ing pictures in real world scenarios. Because of camera and
object motion and due to the ﬁnite exposure interval, the
image of different scene points gets averaged on the sensor
causing motion blur (see Fig. 1 left). Motion deblurring has
been exhaustively studied for the case when blur is uniform
across the image. In this scenario, blind deconvolution algo-
rithms achieve very good performance [37, 28]. However,
in practice, motion blur varies across an image due to ob-
ject motion, camera shake and the 3D structure of the scene.
Modeling the relationship between the blurred observation
and the sharp latent image by considering all these factors,
in addition to occlusion effects, is quite a complicated task.
Consequently, algorithms that handle space-variant blur ei-
ther address only speciﬁc scenarios [15, 30, 40] or simplify
the blur model [21, 36].
The common approach followed by deblurring algo-
rithms is to initially determine the point spread function
(PSF) that describes the blur and then solve for the sharp
image [27, 26, 28]. Since the space-variant PSF is directly
linked with the unknown factors such as scene depth, cam-
era motion and segmentation boundaries of objects, deter-

mining it in a general setting is quite challenging. In this
paper, we follow a different approach. Rather than enforc-
ing a model to relate the blurred observation and the sharp
image, we attempt to directly recover the latent sharp image
by discriminative learning through a deep network formula-
tion. We learn the parameters of the mapping function from
real data and avoid an explicit model in terms of the PSF.
A large amount of data is required for training the network
that represents the mapping function. One way to obtain
this data is to create blurred-unblurred image pairs by syn-
thetically blurring images. However, these images do not
capture effects such as real object and camera motion and
occlusions. A system to obtain such data would in princi-
ple require a hybrid setup with 2 synchronized cameras, one
with a long exposure and one with a short one. However, to
avoid such setup altogether, we use a high frame rate video
camera. The sharp image is taken as one of the frames and
the blurry image as the average of all the frames. Fig. 1
shows an example of the ability of our network to generate
sharp images despite space-variant blur.
Our contributions are: 1) A state-of-the-art blind decon-
volution method for general space-varying motion blur (due
to moving objects, depth variations and camera shake); 2) A
novel convolutional network design motivated by the pyra-
mid architecture; 3) The sharp image is directly obtained
from our network very efﬁciently and without parameter
tuning; 4) A novel database for sharp-motion blurred im-
age pairs in the wild, which we will make available.
1.1. Related work
With the success of the variational Bayesian approach
of Fergus et al. [9], a large number of blind deconvolu-
tion algorithms have been developed for motion deblur-
ring [2, 5, 26, 37, 31, 28, 45, 42]. Although blind decon-
volution algorithms consider blur to be uniform across the
image, some of the methods are able to handle small varia-
tions due to camera shake [24]. Techniques based on blind
deconvolution have been adapted to address blur variations
due to camera rotations by deﬁning the blur kernel on a
higher dimensional space [11, 13, 40]. Another approach to
handle camera shake induced space-variant blur is through
region-wise blur kernel estimation [12, 19]. In 3D scenes,
motion blur at a pixel is also related to depth at a point. To
address this problem, techniques have been proposed that
initially estimate a depth map and then solve for the motion
blur PSF and the sharp image [16, 43]. In [46], motion blur
due to forward or backward camera motion has been explic-
itly addressed. Note that all the above approaches consider
blurring due to camera motion and are not suitable to handle
sudden variations in blur due to moving objects.
Comparatively fewer methods exist for deblurring dy-
namic scenes.
In an initial attempt to this problem by
Levin [25], the blurred image is considered to be composed
of different regions within which blur is assumed to be con-
stant. Based on the effect of blur on image statistics, the
segmentation map and blur estimates for different regions
are initially derived.
Subsequently, each region is sepa-
rately deblurred through Richardson-Lucy deconvolution.
Couzini et al. [6] perform deblurring for both space vari-
ant defocus and horizontal motion blur. They propose to
estimate the space-variant PSF by combining learned local
blur evidence with global smoothness constraints and then
recover the sharp image using a deconvolution algorithm
based on sparse regularization with global image statistics.
The main restriction in these methods is that the blur model
does not accurately represent the camera or object motion.
Techniques based on alpha matting have been applied to re-
store scenes with two layers [7, 39]. Although these meth-
ods can handle moving objects, they require user interaction
and cannot be used in general scenarios where blur varies
due to camera motion and scene depth.
Kim et al. [20] proposed a segmentation-based technique
to deblur dynamic scenes. The method incorporates alter-
nate minimization to estimate blur kernels, latent image,
and motion segments. Even with a general camera shake
model for blurring, the algorithm fails in certain scenarios
such as forward motion or depth variations [21]. In the tech-
nique proposed in [21] by Kim and Lee, abrupt changes
in motion are handled without segmentation by a robust
model. To reduce the complexity, motion blur is modeled
by linear ﬁlters (uniform velocity assumption). The authors
propose to simultaneously estimate motion ﬂow and the la-
tent image using a robust total variation (TV-L1) model.
Schelten and Roth [32] propose a method to estimate and
remove local non-parametric blur. Through a variational-
Bayesian formulation, their framework recovers both defo-
cus as well as object motion blur kernels. The sharp image
is estimated by non-blind deblurring. Pan et al. [29] propose
an efﬁcient algorithm to jointly estimate object segmenta-
tion and camera motion by incorporating soft segmentation.
In addition to the unknowns of sharp image and blur kernel
of each layer, they introduce the terms of binary mask and
a segmentation conﬁdence map in their framework that al-
lows handling of large blurs. Although the method produces
good results in very challenging scenarios, the downside is
that it requires user input for initializing the segmentation
map and the number of segments. Another related approach
to the deblurring problem is to use multiple frames. Recent
works following this approach are able handle space-variant
blur quite well [17, 41]. The works in [4, 10, 35] address
the problem of segmenting an image into different regions
according to blur. Although one could potentially estimate
constant blur regions from these segmentation methods and
apply standard deblurring techniques, in practice, such ap-
proaches would lead to artifacts at the boundaries unless
handled suitably.

Since deep learning-based approaches have been able to
achieve remarkable performance in different problems in
computer vision, researchers have attempted to use them
for motion deblurring. The methods in [33, 44] address the
non-blind deconvolution problem wherein the sharp image
is predicted using the PSF estimated from other techniques.
In [34], Schuler et al. develop an end-to-end system that
learns to perform blind deconvolution. Their system con-
sists of modules to extract features, estimate the PSF and
to perform deblurring. Similar to conventional blind de-
convolution algorithm, they incorporate multi-scale itera-
tions by repeating the set of modules. The performance
of this approach degrades for larger sizes of blur kernels.
Chakrabarti [3] proposes to learn the prediction of com-
plex Fourier coefﬁcients of a deconvolution ﬁlter for an in-
put patch of the blurry image. From the restored patches, a
global blur kernel and thence the sharp image are estimated
through conventional optimization approaches. Hradiˇs et
al. [14] predict clean and sharp images from text documents
that are corrupted by motion blur, defocus and noise through
a convolutional network without an explicit PSF estima-
tion.
This approach has been extended to license plates
in [38]. While textual data contains repeated shapes and
good data representation can be learnt for reconstruction,
replicating this approach for natural images is signiﬁcantly
challenging. The deep learning-based methods discussed
above are applicable for uniform blur scenarios.
Sun et
al. [36] propose to addresses non-uniform motion blur rep-
resented in terms of motion vectors with a speciﬁc length
and orientation. They estimate probabilities of local blur
kernels through a ConvNet and subsequently estimate dense
motion kernels by enforcing motion smoothness. Finally
space-variant deblurring is performed using patch-level im-
age prior. Handling object motion could be difﬁcult with
this scheme because of the motion smoothness constraint.

the next section. Furthermore, this network requires a large
set of blurry and sharp image pairs. To this end, we gener-
ate a new dataset using high resolution videos of dynamic
scenes. The use of such a dataset is critical for achieving
good performance as later seen in the experimental section.
We describe the dataset generation more in detail in Sec. 3.
2.1. The Multiscale Convolutional Neural Network
Our convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture,
shown in Fig. 2, has two main differences with respect to
conventional CNNs. These differences play a signiﬁcant
role in generating high quality sharp images without arti-
facts.
Firstly, it is designed in a pyramid or multi-scale
fashion. Inspired by the multi-scale processing of blind de-
convolution algorithms [28, 34], we include three segments
N1, N2, and N3 in our network, wherein each segment in-
cludes several convolution/deconvolution (fractional convo-
lution) layers. The task of each segment is to minimize the
reconstruction error at a particular scale. Because the extent
of blur decreases with downsampling [28], the multi-scale
formulation allows the network to deal with small amounts
of blur in each segment. In particular, the task for the ﬁrst
segment N1 is to generate a deblurred image at 1/4 of the
original scale. The task for the segment N2 is to start from
this low resolution, but sharper, input and generate a sharp
image at 1/2 of the original resolution. Finally, the task for
the segment N3 is to generate the sharp output at the orig-
inal resolution. Secondly, the network is designed with a
skip connection at the end of each segment. The idea behind
this technique is to reduce the difﬁculty of the reconstruc-
tion task in the network by using the information already
present in the blurry image. Thus, each segment needs to
only generate a residual image which is then added to the

3. Blurry Images in the Wild
We train our network with a realistic dataset consisting of
sharp and blurry observations of a dynamic scene obtained
through a high resolution video camera. We average a set
of frames to obtain realistic blurred observations. Similar
averaging of frames has been done in previous works to ob-
tain data for evaluation [22, 1]. While in [22] a blurry video
is simulated, in [1] blurry images, coded exposure images
and motion invariant photographs are synthesized.
From a handheld GoPro Hero5 camera which has a
frame rate of 240 fps with a resolution of 1280×720, we
collected a large set of videos of outdoor scenes. We down-
sampled all the frames in the videos by a factor of three
in order to reduce the magnitude of relative motion across
frames. The number of frames Ne to be averaged is selected
at random from a set of odd numbers ranging between 7 and
23. Out of the Ne frames, the central frame is considered to
be the sharp image. The averaging of frames corresponds
to a discrete version of the integration in Eqn. (1). How-
ever, if the velocity of the relative motion is high, a scene
point could jump more than 1 pixel across frames. Such
jumps would not be occurring in a real motion blurred im-
age. To avoid frames with higher displacement, we evaluate
optical ﬂow using the recent FlowNet algorithm [8]. We do
not include those sets of frames in our dataset if the opti-
cal ﬂow between adjacent frames is more than a pixel at
any location. Fig. 4 shows an example of the sharp and
blurred image pair in our training dataset. In this scene, we
ﬁnd both the camera and objects to be moving. By simply
averaging the frames, we can embed the information corre-
sponding to motion parallax, occlusions camera shake and

4. Experiments
We tested the performance of our method on three dif-
ferent types of data: a) a test set in the wild created from a
high frame rate video camera, b) real images captured from
a Canon EOS 5D, and c) data from previous works. Addi-
tional results and comparisons are presented in the supple-
mentary material.
To verify the impact of using a realistic dataset for space-
variant blurring, we trained another network with the same
architecture as the one in Fig. 2. However, we used synthet-
ically blurred images according to the convolution model.
As in [3], we prepared a set of 100K different PSFs. Dur-
ing training, we randomly pick a motion PSF and convolve
it with a sharp texture to generate blurred data. We refer
to this network as the MSCNN(SI), multiscale CNN space-
invariant (data), and include comparisons of this network
with our method. A second network is instead trained only
on the blurry-sharp image pairs from our dataset in the wild.
This network is called MSCNN(WILD), multiscale CNN
from data in the wild.

7. Analysis
Because our network is very deep we analyze
only the very last layer and leave the analysis of
the other layers to future work. Our network gen-
erates a residual image that when added to the
blurry input yields the sharp image. The blurrier
the image, the larger residual, as more changes
will be required. Thus, in the ideal case, blur is
related to the magnitude of the residual, which
we also call activation.
We therefore expect a
good correlation between our generated residual
and the blur magnitude of the input blurry image.
Our experiments validate this hypothesis quan-
titatively and qualitatively. We take 700 images
of the test set of our dataset, feed them to the net-

work, and calculate their activations, i.e., the l1
norm of the network outputs (the residuals). In
Fig. 18 we show 6 most activating images, i.e.,
images with the highest l1 norm in the output, and
in Fig. 19 we show 6 least activating images, i.e.,
images with lowest l1 norm in the output. In these
images each of the three columns contains two ex-
amples. The image in the ﬁrst row is the input, the
second row shows the estimated residual (the net-
work output), the third row is the deblurred image
(ﬁrst row + second row), and ﬁnally the forth row
is the ground truth. As expected, the most acti-
vating images are highly blurred, while the least
activating ones are sharp samples.
We also show quantitatively that there is a nice
correlation between blurriness and the residual
magnitude. As we have mentioned in the main pa-
per, our dataset stores an estimate of point spread
functions (PSFs) obtained from optical ﬂow for
each blurry image. We calculate the average PSF
size across the blurry image, and this gives us a
reasonable approximation of blurriness (the ap-
proximation is due to the error in the optical ﬂow
estimates).
In Fig. 9 we show the plot of the
l1 norm of the residual versus average estimated
PSF size from optical ﬂow for all 700 images.
The residual magnitudes and blur sizes are nor-
malized so that mean and std of both are 0 and 1
respectively. Notice the high level of correlation
between the blur size and the activation.
