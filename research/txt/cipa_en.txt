1. Introduction 
 
As digital cameras have permeated the market in recent years, new functions have successively been 
incorporated into new cameras to make them more convenient for users. One such function 
commercialized in many cameras is image stabilization, which detects camera shake and corrects 
image blur. The advantages of image stabilization performance are presented in brochures for equipped 
products. To date, however, the industry has not developed any unified methods for measuring or 
describing the image stabilization performance of cameras. This has made it difficult to compare image 
stabilization performance between cameras based on their brochures. 
To date, the Camera and Imaging Products Association (CIPA) has established standards and 
guidelines for digital cameras, battery life, resolution, sensitivity and brochure descriptions. Brochures 
of many digital cameras are prepared in compliance with these standards and guidelines, making for 
fairer competition. 
In the context of this background, this standard in 2012 added measurement and description methods 
for image stabilization performance of digital cameras to existing standards and guidelines. In the 
2015 revision, the standard added description methods for image stabilization performance for 
cameras with image stabilization in directions other than yaw and pitch. In 2024, in response to 
subsequent changes in photographic viewing environments, this standard is revised to allow for 
measurement and description of roll image stabilization performance in addition to yaw and pitch. 
 
This standard specifies the methods with regard to image stabilization as a type of blur suppression 
as defined in 3-1-2; however, it does not specify methods with regard to blur reduction or motion blur 
correction. 
Typical systems of image stabilization include optical and electronic systems. In both systems, 
exposure time (nearly equal to shutter speed) is an important factor. Electronic image stabilization, 
however, has diverged into a broad spectrum of systems, many of which do not have clear definition 
of exposure time, making it difficult to measure image stabilization performance on the basis of this 
standard. 
Hence, electronic image stabilization systems and hybrid image stabilization systems that use both 
optical and electronic image stabilization are excluded from the application of this standard as 
measurements are highly likely to be questionable. 
 
In addition, for the purpose of supplementing the contents of this standard, an explanation of 
terminology relating to "handheld blur" is provided in the appendix.

2. Scope 
 
This standard applies to digital cameras for consumers with optical image stabilization for still 
image shooting, with the following provisions: 
・ Professional or industrial applications in which users and suppliers arrange case-by-case 
specifications are excluded. 
・ Applications with electronic image stabilization systems and with hybrid image stabilization 
systems that use both electronic and optical image stabilization are excluded. 
・ Apparatuses such as camcorders and mobile phones with still image shooting ability shall not 
be precluded from adopting this standard. 
This standard is mainly meant for application in describing image stabilization performance in 
brochures or other printed media and websites that specify the product. However, the objectives of this 
standard are also to be complied with in notation on camera bodies and individual packages, as well 
as expressions used in advertising campaigns and point-of-purchase advertising for sales promotions. 
Furthermore, any party other than suppliers taking measurements based on this standard (hereinafter 
called “measurer”) shall not be precluded from publishing the results of their measurements in their 
own publication.

3. Definition of Terms and Referenced Standards 
 
3-1 Definition of Terms 
The terms used in this standard are defined as given below. 
 
3-1-1 Blur 
"Blur" means deviation from a predetermined position. Used as a camera term, blur is broadly 
divided into handheld blur and motion blur. 
 
1) Handheld Blur  
“Handheld blur” refers to a movement of a camera caused by unstable hands holding the camera, 
resulting in blurring of a subject in an image taken. 
 
2) Motion Blur  
“Motion blur” refers to blurring of a subject in an image caused by movement of the subject or a 
part of the subject during exposure, irrespective of any handheld blur. 
 
3-1-2 Blur Suppression 
“Blur suppression” refers to suppression of handheld blur or motion blur by some means of control 
within a camera. Although blur suppression can be further classified into image stabilization and blur 
reduction, this standard excludes applications for blur reduction. 
Additionally, while image stabilization can be further classified into handheld blur correction and 
motion blur correction, this standard excludes applications for motion blur correction. 
 
1) Image Stabilization 
This function corrects for handheld blur in the output image as caused by movement of the camera 
body due to handheld blur (see 3-3-6) by using output by the blur detection device. 
However, methods ultimately meant primarily to increase shutter speed by optimizing an exposure 
control program are considered "blur reduction" as described below, even when using output by the 
blur detection device. 
 
2) Blur Reduction 
“Blur reduction” is a function or mode for reducing handheld blur in an output image as caused by 
either subject movement or handheld blur primarily by optimizing an exposure control program to 
increase shutter speed. 
Image stabilization and blur reduction shall not be confused, as there are great differences in their

3-1-3 Vibratory apparatus 
A “vibratory apparatus” is an apparatus capable of generating vibration in response to the amplitude 
of input vibration waveforms. Typically, the camera to be measured is mounted on the movable part 
of the vibratory apparatus using given fixing methods to transmit the vibration to it.  
 
3-1-4 Vibration Waveform 
A “vibration waveform” is an embodiment of camera shake (vibration) that occurs when shooting 
a subject with a camera by temporally shifting the amplitude value. A vibration waveform adopted in 
this standard has three axes: yaw and pitch, which are vibration components especially dominant in 
an image taken, and roll, which is a vibration component having the next most significant impact. 
As the characteristics of camera shake vary depending on camera mass, two types of vibration 
waveforms shall be employed: WB-H and WB-L. (For the definition of "weight," see CIPA DCG-005 
Measurement and Description Methods of Weight and Dimensions of Digital Cameras.) 
The WB-H and WB-L files are in text format, named WB-H-xxx.txt and WB-L-xxx.txt, respectively. 
The waveforms are provided as angle data changing in the time direction for a length of 32 seconds. 
Data sampling frequency is 500Hz. The ‘xxx’ in the waveform file names is a 3-digit number giving 
the waveform version. If a waveform has been revised, version differences are managed with these 
numbers. 
 
3-1-5 Handheld Blur Amount 
“Handheld blur amount” is an amount that corresponds to shift of a subject in an image taken as 
induced by handheld blur. 
 
3-1-6 Image Degradation and Image Degradation Amount 
“Image degradation” refers to a phenomenon where deviation between the focal plane of a lens and 
image plane of the image sensor or where handheld blur deteriorates the sharpness in an image taken.

Image degradation is sometimes caused by image processing of image data. “Image degradation 
amount” is a quantified value of the magnitude of image degradation. 
 
3-1-7 Handheld Blur Measurement Chart 
A “handheld blur measurement chart” is a chart to be used as a subject in measuring image 
stabilization performance. When establishing this standard, CIPA prepared a handheld blur 
measurement chart called the “CIPA handheld blur measurement chart.” 
 
3-1-8 Handheld Blur Measurement Software 
"Handheld blur measurement software" is provided by CIPA and dedicated software for measuring 
the intrinsic image degradation amount (3-1-10) and the total image degradation amount (3-1-14) from 
a captured image using the CIPA handheld blur measurement chart (3-1-7). “Pixel” is the unit for the 
image degradation amount generated by this software.   
 
3-1-9 Stop (Stop Number) 
According to APEX expressions, the difference in TV values is represented in ”stops.” For instance, 
the difference between shutter speeds of 1/1000 (TV10) and 1/500 seconds (TV9) or that between 
shutter speeds of 1/125 (TV7) and 1/60 seconds (TV6) accounts for one stop. 
 
3-1-10 Intrinsic image degradation amount 
“Intrinsic image degradation amount” is the image degradation amount of an image taken caused 
by factors other than handheld blur. Intrinsic image degradation amount is unique to an individual 
instrument and is dependent on the optical performance, effective number of pixels and image 
processing of the camera to be measured. By removing intrinsic image degradation amount, almost all 
factors affecting image stabilization performance other than the image stabilization function can be 
removed. 
 
3-1-11 Determination Level for Image Stabilization Performance  
“Determination level for image stabilization performance” is the level used in judgments of image 
stabilization performance calculations. In this standard, this level is defined as 20 μm. 
 
3-1-12 Theoretical Handheld Blur Amount 
“Theoretical handheld blur amount” is a theoretical value for the amount of measurable handheld 
blur in an image taken with image stabilization turned OFF (the function is assumed to be OFF for 
cameras without an OFF setting) when the camera to be measured is vibrated with a vibration 
waveform.

6 
 
 
3-1-13 Reference Image Degradation Amount 
“Reference image degradation amount” is the theoretical estimated image degradation amount for 
an image taken with image stabilization turned OFF (the function is assumed to be OFF for cameras 
without an OFF setting) when the camera to be measured is vibrated with a vibration waveform. This 
amount is expressed as the square root of the sum of the squares of the intrinsic image degradation 
amount and the theoretical handheld blur amount. 
 
3-1-14 Total Image Degradation Amount 
“Total image degradation amount” is the actual measurement value of image degradation amount 
for an image taken with image stabilization turned ON when the camera to be measured is vibrated 
with a vibration waveform. 
 
3-1-15 Reference Handheld Blur Amount 
“Reference handheld blur amount” is a reference value for use in calculating image stabilization 
performance. The reference handheld blur amount is calculated by subtracting the intrinsic image 
degradation amount from the reference image degradation amount caused when the camera is vibrated 
for cameras not equipped with image stabilization or with image stabilization turned OFF (the function 
is assumed to be OFF for cameras without an OFF setting). 
 
3-1-16 Measured Handheld Blur Amount 
“Measured handheld blur amount” is a value representing residual handheld blur after correction 
with image stabilization of the camera to be measured ON. This value is calculated by subtracting 
intrinsic image degradation amount from total image degradation amount. 
 
3-1-17 Average Vibration Angle  
“Average vibration angle” is the average value for vibration angles caused by vibration with a 
vibration waveform (3-1-4) at each shutter speed.   
Average vibration angle data files are in text format, with the file corresponding to WB-H named 
AVA-1-xxx.txt and that corresponding to WB-L named AVA-2-xxx.txt. The file corresponding to 
Selection Criteria III described in 4-2-6 is named AVA-3-xxx.txt. 
Again, the ‘xxx’ in the file names is a 3-digit number giving the version number. The 3 digits are 
used to manage the files, corresponding with the waveform revisions. 
 
3-1-18 Shutter Speed 
“Shutter speed” is a theoretical exposure time value.

4. Measurement Method 
 
4-1 Preconditions 
 The objective of this standard is to specify how to measure image stabilization performance in the 
case of users taking a shot with the camera in hand. Accordingly, measuring sessions will better 
simulate real shooting if the camera to be measured is actually held by hand. This does, however, make 
it difficult to eliminate variations due to differences among individual photographers or how well the 
camera is designed for handheld shooting. In order to ensure fair measurement results, the camera to 
be measured is to be mounted on a vibratory apparatus to vibrate the camera with a vibration waveform 
to be discussed later, and image stabilization performance shall be measured on the basis of results of 
shooting the handheld blur measurement chart specified in this standard. 
 
Information on how to get information and tools (vibration waveforms, handheld blur measurement 
software, CIPA handheld blur measurement chart, etc.) additionally required for the measurement of 
image stabilization performance as specified in this standard shall be separately presented by CIPA. 
 
Measurements of image stabilization performance shall comply with the following: 4-2 Equipment 
and Environment for Measurement, 4-3 Setting of Camera to be Measured and 4-4 Measurement 
Procedures. Determinations on the number of cameras to be measured is left to the supplier or the 
measurer, as long as the number does not render questionable measurement results. Figure 4-1 shows 
the overview of the measurement method.

4-2 Equipment and Environment for Measurement 
 
4-2-1 Handheld Blur Measurement Chart 
For this standard, a handheld blur measurement chart satisfying the following specifications shall 
be used for the measurement of image stabilization performance. 
1) The chart shall be comprised of measurement sites at the center of the image and positions at 
image height 60%, and may optionally include a portion having natural color images. 
2) The ratio of reflectance between the white and black regions of the measurement sites shall be 
4:1 or higher, and the white and black region shall be wider than the expected maximum image 
degradation amount. 
3) No particular features are specified for the natural image portion, but the portion preferably 
contains images similar to real subjects. 
 
Use of the CIPA handheld blur measurement chart illustrated in Figure 4-2-1a is recommended. The 
CIPA handheld blur measurement chart has natural color images inserted to facilitate focusing or 
general purpose applications. The outline dimensions of the CIPA handheld blur measurement chart 
are 1000 mm × 750 mm in consideration of shooting magnification. 
White and black edge areas for measurement are provided at the center and at four locations at 
image height 60% within the frames indicating the shooting range. To ensure measurement at corner 
positions of image height 60% even with differing aspect ratios of imaging elements, the chart has 
three frames (aspect ratios of 3:2, 4:3, and 16:9); therefore, the camera's shooting range shall be 
adjusted in accordance with the format of the imaging element so that the frame lines substantially 
match the screen edges to be shot. The lens focal length shall then be determined and the shooting 
distance adjusted. This process is assumed in the descriptions of this standard below.

4-2-2 Lighting 
Although no specific types of lighting are defined, lighting shall not cause flicker in the image 
during shooting. Measures should also be taken to prevent reflection from the light source or uneven 
illuminance on the handheld blur measurement chart to prevent any question relating to measurement 
results. 
Lighting with variable brightness is useful. 
 
4-2-3 Temperature and Humidity 
The temperature and humidity shall be 23°C ± 2°C and 30 to 70%. Measurements may be performed 
under other conditions as long as there is no room for question. 
 
4-2-4 Vibratory Apparatus 
The vibratory apparatus to use shall function in a manner that accurately reproduces vibrations 
according to the vibration waveforms. Always verify that the vibratory apparatus is vibrating 
accurately before taking measurements. To do so, measurers of image stabilization performance or 
manufacturers of vibratory apparatuses refer to Verification Method of Vibratory Apparatus 
specified separately. 
 
4-2-5 Mounting of Camera to be Measured on Vibratory Apparatus 
When mounting the camera to be measured on the vibratory apparatus, vibration of the vibratory 
apparatus and that of the camera to be measured mounted on the vibratory apparatus have to match.  
When measuring a camera with a long-barrel lens (e.g. high-powered zoom lens), vibrations of the 
camera body and the lens may not match due to distortion induced in the lens by excitation preventing 
the applied vibration from being correctly transmitted to the lens. Thus, given measures are to be taken 
for such measurements so that lens and camera body vibrations match, such as fixing the lens to the 
vibratory apparatus in addition to the camera body. 
The vibration waveforms adopted in this standard are vibrations that rotate on the rotational axes of 
yaw, pitch, and roll; therefore, the positional relationship between the center of rotation of the vibratory 
apparatus and the optical axis of the camera and lens to be measured at the time of mounting affects 
the vibration measurement results. (Hereafter, camera and lens are abbreviated as camera.)

4-2-6 Vibration Waveform 
There are two types of vibration waveforms: WB-L and WB-H. Criteria for selecting a waveform 
are as follows: (Here, total mass is the sum of the masses of a camera body, the included storage media 
and battery, and a lens) 
Selection Criterion I. WB-H shall be used for total mass of 600 g or more; 
Selection Criterion II. WB-L shall be used for total mass of less than 400 g; 
Selection Criterion III. Both WB-L and WB-H shall be used for total mass between 400 g and 600 
g. 
Both waveforms consist of 3-axis components: yaw, pitch, and roll. Yaw, pitch, and roll components 
shall be excited at the same time. 
 
(Note: Given that camera geometries and shooting methods change and image stabilization 
techniques will be improved in response to such changes, the vibration waveforms specified in this 
standard are subject to revision as necessary.)

 When the vibratory apparatus is stopped, the shooting distance shall be adjusted so that the white 
and black edges at image height 60% at the four positions in the CIPA handheld blur measurement 
chart in Figure 4-2-1a are within an image height of 60 ± 3%. This distance is 20 times the 35mm film 
equivalent focal length. The objective of the adjustment is to ensure that the image is taken so that the 
range of the frame indicated for each aspect ratio depicted in Figure 4-2-1a CIPA Handheld Blur 
Measurement Chart covers the entire screen area when the vibratory apparatus is stopped.

4-4 Measurement Procedures 
 
4-4-1 Measurement of Intrinsic image degradation amount: 
1) Make preparations on the basis of environment settings described in 4-2 and 4-3; 
2) Mount the camera to be measured on the vibratory apparatus; 
3) Turn the power of the camera to be measured ON. For cameras mounted with a zoom lens, set 
the lens to the focal length for image stabilization performance measurement. Here, in 
accordance with the image aspect ratio of the camera to be measured, adjust the shooting 
distance and the position of the camera to be measured or the position of the CIPA handheld 
blur measurement chart so that the portion enclosed by the frame indicating the corresponding 
aspect ratio in Figure 4-2-1a fills the entire screen during shooting; 
4) The shutter speed shall be set around ”1/ focal length (35 mm film equivalent)”;

5. Description Method 
5-1 Overview 
This chapter specifies the description methods. 
In the establishment of this standard, two vibration waveforms (WB-H and WB-L) are specified 
which quite faithfully simulate characteristics of camera shake as induced by handheld shooting. 
These two vibration waveforms were developed by analyzing extensive measurement data and 
adding further theoretical considerations. 
In real situations, however, shooting generally starts by taking the camera out of a pocket or bag, 
adjusting certain settings, determining the shot composition by looking through a viewfinder or LCD 
monitor, and then pressing the shutter button. Thus, cameras tend to be subjected to large vibrations 
and shaking other than camera shake that arise during shooting. For some cameras, such factors may 
adversely affect the image stabilization performance at the instant of shooting which this standard is 
intended to address. Still, faithfully reproducing the adverse effect such vibrations have on 
measurements is not easy under this standard using a vibratory apparatus. It is also technically possible 
to produce a camera that is intentionally designed to generate favorable measurement results without 
regard to practicality with special settings that are adapted to the characteristics of the camera vibration 
waveforms specified in this standard. 
Image stabilization performance in such cameras would be significantly lower than presented in the 
brochure or other documentation in actual handheld shooting. Accordingly, if the image stabilization 
performance for such cameras is presented in their brochures or the like as measured under this 
standard without modification, it would be misleading and detrimental to users. Therefore, suppliers 
shall take great care not to make any presentation of image stabilization performance that may be 
misleading or detrimental to users. If the image stabilization performance presented in the brochure or 
the like is only available under specific conditions, such conditions shall be expressly stated unless 
such conditions or facts are easily attainable by users from other information. 
 
Performance and other specifications of the camera shall be measured according to the setup given 
in 4. Measurement Method in this standard and described based on the specifications in 5. Description 
Method. Here, description of measurement conditions and other information may be omitted except 
where mandatory. 
Description of values measured using methods other than those in this standard is not prohibited, 
but such descriptions shall be made less prominently and measurement conditions shall be described. 
If a numerical value to be described is an integer, its fractional portion may be omitted, 
irrespective of digits specified for each item. (Example: 2.0 stops ⇒ 2 stops) 
Specific names, designations, Japanese long-sound symbols, and distinctions between single-byte 
and double-byte expressions may be used in customary expressions or names used by the suppliers or 
DRAFT

35 
 
measurers provided that they do not cause misunderstanding. It shall be noted that:  
Items clearly specified to be uniformly used and names defined in this standard shall follow such 
specifications; and names that may be confused with those defined in this standard shall not be used 
under a different definition. 
 
The method of describing image stabilization performance in brochures shall be as follows. 
Image stabilization performance shall be described using "stops." Values shall be rounded to the 
nearest 0.5-stop (e.g., 2.7 stops → 2.5 stops) and described as shown in the following example. Also, 
image stabilization performance may be described for multiple focal lengths. Here, 
a) The factory default setting is recommended for image stabilization mode. When image 
stabilization performance is described by itself in this mode, the name of the image 
stabilization mode used in the measurement may be omitted. 
b) When using an image stabilization mode other than the factory default setting, the name of the 
image stabilization mode used for measurement shall be clearly described.

6. Explanation 
 
6-1 Background of Specifying Measurement Method 
This chapter specifies measurement methods in an effort to contribute to sound development within 
the industry through the realization of fair competition. 
When measuring image stabilization performance, the most understandable way is to compare the 
results with image stabilization OFF against those with image stabilization ON and quantitatively 
describe the difference as the effect of image stabilization. When doing so, the intrinsic image 
degradation amount is first removed from the figures for both the ON and OFF states before 
comparison. This allows for image degradation elements caused by factors other than camera shake to 
be easily removed and produces fairer measurement results. 
Meanwhile, in the measurement method for this standard, the camera to be measured is vibrated 
using a vibratory apparatus with the vibration waveforms specified by the standard and prescribed 
performance. Accordingly, handheld blur amounts with image stabilization OFF should theoretically 
be similar among cameras, exhibiting only subtle individual differences. Results of measurements for 
this standard performed on many cameras have confirmed that in actuality, there is no significant 
difference among cameras. 
With fully automated cameras already on the market, there are already cameras without a mode 
where image stabilization is OFF. This makes it difficult to measure all the cameras based on whether 
image stabilization is ON or OFF. This trend is predicted to grow even further in the future. 
Considering the errors that occur with measurement and the man-hours involved, completing 
measurement with image stabilization ON only instead of measuring twice with image stabilization 
both ON and OFF will cut the chances for measurement errors in half and also reduce the work load. 
For the above reasons, in this standard, image stabilization performance is calculated from the 
reference handheld blur amount, which corresponds to measurement results with image stabilization 
OFF, and measured handheld blur amount, which is the measurement result with image stabilization 
ON. 
 
 
6-2 Vibratory Apparatus 
In measuring a camera’s image stabilization performance, the most realistic scenario is to shoot the 
handheld blur measurement chart with the camera by hand. The scale and characteristics of camera 
shake will vary among different individuals, however, and the same person may even produce different 
results with a different camera or shooting conditions. 
To specify the methods for measurement and describe image stabilization performance, such 
uncertainties shall be eliminated. Using a mechanical device to generate vibration simulating camera

shake and transmitting the vibration to the camera is an effective means of achieving this affect. Such 
devices are called vibratory apparatuses. A vibratory apparatus is designed to reproduce camera shake 
by human hands with an electric signal called a vibration waveform applied to vibrate the movable 
part of the apparatus.

6-3 Vibration Waveform 
Preferably, the vibration waveform will be as close as possible to camera shake characteristics. 
Accordingly, CIPA has collected camera shake frequencies and amplitudes from many people by 
attaching a sensor to a camera and separating them into yaw, pitch and roll. Vibration waveforms were 
generated by analyzing the collected data and extracting characteristic frequency and amplitude to 
synthesize the waveforms. 
Handheld blur has six directional components in total: yaw, pitch, roll, X, Y, and Z. At a shooting 
distances of about 20 times the focal length, however, yaw and pitch followed by roll are dominant, 
making the other directions practically negligible. Thus, waveforms with only three-axis components 
were adopted.

segments: WB-H for interchangeable lens cameras and WB-L for compact cameras.

6-6 Shooting Distance 
In principle, the distance between the handheld blur measurement chart and the camera to be 
measured when measuring image stabilization performance shall be about 20 times the 35 mm film 
equivalent focal length. This distance has been calculated in light of practical aspects to minimize 
doubt about measurement results and the experiment environment. As an example, the shooting 
distance for a camera with a 35 mm film equivalent focal length of 100 mm is about 2 m. 
For a wide-angle lens having a 35 mm film equivalent focal length of 24 mm, the shooting distance 
will be no more than 500 mm. In this situation, the handheld blur measurement chart, vibratory 
apparatus, and lighting device can interfere, making measurement difficult. Conversely, the shooting 
distance for a telephoto lens having a 35 mm film equivalent focal length of at least 500 mm would be 
10 m or more, which could possibly make a shooting site with the proper conditions impractical. 
In such unavoidable cases, shooting distance may be increased or shortened from 20 times the 35 
mm film equivalent focal length by the minimum amount required.

6-7 Number of Shots 
The vibration waveforms shown in 6-3 are used to measure image stabilization performance in this 
standard. However, there may be image stabilization performance errors due to bias in the data 
collected if not enough shots are taken. 
Thus, the specified minimum number of shots for each shutter speed in this standard is 200. 
Shooting experiments (shooting at a shutter speed of 1/8 second) on many test subjects have 
statistically revealed that taking roughly 200 images can suppress 95% of the variations in every 
measurement of total image degradation amount to the determination level for image stabilization 
performance or less. 
 
 
6-8 Determination Level for Image Stabilization 
With handheld blur amount from images being the variable measured to determine the image 
stabilization performance, it can be challenging to measure performance when there is only a little 
image degradation or handheld blur. 
In photography, the diameter of what is called the circle of confusion is often used for determining 
image degradation. The largest circle of confusion incapable of determining image degradation and 
handheld blur is referred to as the permissible circle of confusion. The size of the permissible circle of 
confusion will differ based on factors such as the size of the picture viewed, viewing distance and the 
basis for calculation. 
Thus, a level was derived by experiment that is close to the size of the permissible circle of 
confusion and leaves no doubts about the performance measurement that is the determination level for 
image stabilization performance. 
In recent years, the viewing environment of photographs has become more diverse, no longer being 
limited to postcard size, and includes A4 or larger prints as well as viewing by computer monitors, 
smartphones, tablets, and television monitors. With such diversity, photographs and images to be 
viewed are expanding more than before. 
Specifically, images were created by enlarging the diameter of the circle of confusion incrementally 
by computer simulation, and then prints of various sizes of A4 and larger were subjected to panel 
testing at viewing distances from which the entire image could be seen. Panel tests were conducted 
using a 24-inch PC monitor as a representative of self-illuminating displays, with the entire image 
displayed at a normal viewing distance. 
Generally, sensory evaluations of image quality will have a reference set at a point where at least 
10% of panelists can recognize the difference in image quality. In the two panel test, approximately

50 
 
10% of panelists could determine image degradation or blur in the permissible circle of confusion at 
a diameter of about 24 μm. 
The permissible circle of confusion for each viewing device was calculated on the basis of the screen 
size and viewing distance. That is, given 1 minute as the angle between two points which a person 
with an eyesight of 1.0 (20/20 vision) is capable of discriminating and 600mm as the viewing distance, 
for example, then, on the viewing device screen, this is equivalent to: 
tan (1/60°) × 600 [mm] × 1000 [μm/mm] = 175 [μm] 
Therefore, image degradation of 175 µm or less on the viewing device display screen can be said to 
be almost unrecognizable by the viewer. 
If this viewing device is an A4 print (diagonal length 364mm) and the camera is a 35mm full size 
(diagonal length 43.3mm) camera, then, on the imaging element,  
175 [μm] × 43.3 [mm] / 364 [mm] = 21 [μm] 
is the equivalent image degradation amount. 
From the above result, the determination level for image stabilization performance was set to 20 
μm.

6-9 Image Stabilization Performance Calculation 
Generally, the measured handheld blur amount rapidly increases as shutter speeds slow down. 
Accordingly, the actual handheld blur amount is a curve connecting handheld blur amounts acquired 
discretely at intervals of at most one stop. While deriving the precise function for this curve would 
require advanced mathematical techniques, linear approximation does not produce significant error as 
long as intervals are at most one stop. Further, as the reference handheld blur amount forms a curve 
similar to that of the measured handheld blur amount, linear approximation of these is expected to 
have the errors in both cancel each other out and further reduce error. 
Thus, this standard avoids using a complex curve for calculating image stabilization performance 
with linear approximation connecting two points with a straight line. 
 
 
6-10 Lighting 
Since the type and color temperature of lighting are unlikely to greatly affect measurement results, 
no type of lighting is specified. However, the full handheld blur measurement chart shall be evenly lit 
during measurement. Accordingly, the number of lights, positioning and other layout details will 
require consideration. 
Brightness of lighting is to be adjustable in case brightness adjustments are required to change the 
shutter speed for settings in which shutter speed cannot be changed easily by camera operation. In 
such cases, combined use with a neutral density (ND) filter can be effective.

6-11 Shooting Mode 
 With regard to the shooting mode for measuring image stabilization performance, while no specific 
shooting mode is stipulated, shooting modes will preferably be capable of remembering as many of 
the items specified in 4-3-3 to 4-3-19 as possible and setting them in advance with the power OFF 
given that shooting shall begin within three seconds after shooting is enabled following power ON.  
This is required because shooting cannot start immediately if camera settings for measurement are 
adjusted after the power is turned ON.  
Next, the option of a mode "allowing for easy changing of the shutter speed" is mentioned. This is 
recommended to make it easier to change shutter speeds when fulfilling the requirement to measure 
handheld blur amounts at each shutter speed. 
Many cameras, however, only have shooting modes such as "Auto" and "Program" that 
automatically change shutter speed and aperture values according to the shooting environment and 
state of the subject. For such cameras, measures will be required for changing the shutter speed, such 
as adjusting the brightness of lighting. 
 
 
6-12 Image Quality Mode (Compression Ratio)  
While no compression ratio is specified, image compression may result in noise effects which raise 
doubts about the measurement results. Accordingly, a high image quality mode with low compression 
ratio is preferable. 
   
 
6-13 Image Quality Mode (Number of Recording Pixels)  
While no number of recording pixels is specified, fewer recording pixels may degrade resolution to 
the point that doubts are raised about the measurement results. Accordingly, taking images at the 
maximum number of pixels offered by the camera as not to impact resolution is preferable. 
 
 
6-14 ISO Sensitivity 
While no ISO sensitivity is specified, the noise effects from shooting at high sensitivity may raise 
doubts about the measurement results. Accordingly, ISO sensitivity should be set relatively low to 
prevent noise. 
However, some cameras do not allow the user to set ISO sensitivity. For such cameras, make every 
effort not to render questionable measurement results. As noise levels change with ISO sensitivity 
changes, camera settings should prevent ISO sensitivity changes where possible.

6-15 Flash 
Flashes may change image quality by emitting light and raise doubts about the measurement results. 
Many cameras do not allow shutter speed adjustments with the flash activated. Accordingly, 
measurement shall be made with the flash OFF. 
 
 
6-16 Storage Media 
Since types and capacities of storage media are not likely to raise issues with measurement results, 
none are specified. However, storage media of extremely small capacity will have to be replaced 
frequently during measurement and will require data to be deleted. This would be a concern as 
changing camera settings may result in inconsistent measurements. 
Accordingly, storage media with sufficiently large capacity, that will not have to be replaced during 
measurements, are more convenient. 
 
 
6-17 Focal Length for Measurements (Cameras Mounted with Zoom Lenses) 
With optical zoom factors and focal length ranges varying from product to product, not every digital 
camera can be measured at the same focal length. At first, the possibility of methods of selecting from 
several predetermined focal lengths at both the wide-angle and telephoto end was considered. Given 
that some cameras cannot select specific focal lengths, the decision on a focal length for measurement 
will be left up to suppliers and measurers. Meanwhile, some cameras also offer different image 
stabilization performance at different focal lengths. For this reason, the focal length for the 
measurement shall be clearly labeled when listing image stabilization performance. 
 
 
6-18 Electronic (Digital) Zoom 
Electronic (digital) zoom shall not be used as it deteriorates image quality, which may raise doubts 
about measurement results. 
 
 
6-19 Focus Control 
Whether autofocus (AF) or manual focus (MF) is used, measurements cannot distinguish between 
handheld blur and the lens being out of focus if the camera is not properly focused on the handheld 
blur measurement chart. This could possibly raise questions about measurement results. 
Accordingly, the camera shall be properly focused on the handheld blur measurement chart when

measuring. 
 
 
6-20 White Balance 
While white balance settings are not specified, shooting with extremely unbalanced color 
temperature settings could raise questions about measurement results. Accordingly, white balance is 
to be set appropriately for the light source to be used. 
 
 
6-21 Image Aspect Ratio 
While some cameras are capable of changing the image aspect ratio during imaging, differences in 
image aspect ratio are unlikely to raise issues with measurement results for image stabilization 
performance. There is thus no need to specify the image aspect ratio. The factory default setting mode 
is recommended. 
 
 
6-22 Image Stabilization Mode 
Cameras may have one of several types of image stabilization modes. Some modes keep image 
stabilization constantly on, while in others image stabilization may only be turned on when the shutter 
is released. Some even have variable image stabilization characteristics depending on the shooting 
mode and zoom position. 
Generally, most cameras will have a factory default auto shooting mode. As suppliers recommend 
shooting mode to be set to auto, image stabilization modes where the camera operates in this state 
should be used. 
In some cases, settings for image stabilization are not available in the factory default shooting mode, 
or image stabilization modes other than the factory default setting may exhibit higher image 
stabilization performance at a certain focal length. In these cases, the supplier or measurer is not 
precluded from selecting the appropriate image stabilization mode. However, if an image stabilization 
mode other than the factory default setting is used, the name of the image stabilization mode used in 
measurement shall be clearly listed.

Camera Shake Characteristics 
 Camera shake has components in six axes total (yaw, pitch, roll, X, Y, and Z). For the sake of 
explanation, assume that there is a left-handed coordinate system in three-dimensional space 
(Cartesian coordinate system with X, Y, and Z axes) and that the optical axis of the imaging lens is 
coincident with the Z axis. 
 The X, Y, and Z components are the handheld blur in which reciprocating translational motion 
occurs in the X-, Y-, and Z-axis directions, respectively. The Z component is the handheld blur in the 
optical axis direction. The remaining X and Y components are the handheld blur in planes 
perpendicular to the optical axis. The vertical and horizontal handheld blur together are generally 
referred to as the shift component or shift handheld blur. 
 The yaw, pitch, and roll components are the handheld blur in which reciprocating rotational motion 
occurs about the X axis, Y axis, and Z axis, respectively. The reciprocating rotational motion about the 
Y axis and the reciprocating rotational motion about the X axis are called the yaw component and the 
pitch component. The reciprocating rotational motion about the optical axis (Z axis) is called the roll 
component. 
 The X, Y, and Z components, when converted to the amounts of handheld blur on an imaging 
surface, are proportional to the magnification of the image. When the shooting distance is 20 times the 
focal length or more, the amount of handheld blur decreases considerably, and the yaw, pitch, and then 
roll components become dominant in the amount of handheld blur on the imaging plane, and the other 
three axis components of X, Y, and Z can be virtually ignored.

6-27 Difference in Number of Stops of Image Stabilization Performance between Center and 
Corner 
 The number of stops of image stabilization performance tends to be lower at the corner than at the 
center. The amount of reduction in the number of stops at the corner relative to the center tends to be 
smaller for telephoto lenses than for wide-angle lenses. The reason is as follows. 
 For ease of explanation, description will be made using a wide-angle lens having focal length of 
24mm and a telephoto lens having focal length of 200mm as examples. 
 
 In principle, there is no roll handheld blur effect at the center. Therefore, the technical difficulty of 
reducing the 200μm combined yaw and pitch handheld blur amount caused by vibration waveforms 
to the handheld blur determination level of 20μm, that is, to 1/10, is about the same for both the 24mm 
wide angle and the 200mm telephoto. 
 
 On the other hand, at the corners (image height 60%), there is a roll handheld blur effect, and the 
shutter speed corresponding to a 200μm combined yaw, pitch, and roll handheld blur amount caused 
by vibration waveforms is approximately 1.5 seconds at 24mm and approximately 1/19 seconds at 
200mm. At this time, the ratio of the roll handheld blur amount to the combined yaw, pitch, and roll 
handheld blur amount and the roll handheld blur amount are 23% and approximately 57μm at 24mm 
and 3% and approximately 7μm at 200mm. 
The yaw and pitch handheld blur amounts are values close to 200μm, and thus the technical 
difficulty of reducing these amounts to 1/10 is the same as that at the center described above. 
However, compared to the yaw and pitch handheld blur amounts, the roll handheld blur amounts 
are as small as 57μm and 7μm, and the technical difficulty of further reducing these small amounts to 
1/10 is high. 
 Therefore, even if it is technically possible to reduce the yaw and pitch handheld blur amounts to 
1/10, it may not be possible to reduce the roll handheld blur amount to 1/10. As a result, the number 
of stops of image stabilization performance tends to be lower at the corners than at the center. 
 The roll handheld blur amounts are 57μm at 24mm and already a minute 7μm at 200mm. Therefore, 
even if the handheld blur amount cannot be reduced to 1/10, the amount of residual handheld blur is 
very small. Thus, with the roll handheld blur amount at 200mm being small to begin with, the residual 
handheld blur is very small even if the blur reduction effect is small. Accordingly, at 200mm, compared 
to 24mm, the reduction in the number of stops of image stabilization performance at the corners tends 
to be small compared to that at the center. 
 In other words, the yaw and pitch handheld blur amounts increase in proportion to focal length, but 
the roll handheld blur amount does not change. As the focal length becomes increasingly telephoto, 
the shutter speed to be measured increases and the yaw and pitch handheld blur amounts and roll

relatively small, and the difference between the numbers of stops at the center and at the corner tends 
to decrease in the telephoto range.

6-28 Vibration Waveforms 
 The 2024 standard DC-X011-2024 revision adds a roll motion blur component to the vibration 
waveforms, resulting in the vibration waveforms having the three rotating components of yaw, pitch, 
and roll. Camera shake caused by the human hand includes components in six axes total (yaw, pitch, 
roll, X, Y, and Z), but the three X, Y, and Z translational components are not addressed herein. Camera 
shake has components in six axes total (yaw, pitch, roll, X, Y, and Z). 
 Ideally, the vibration waveforms should be similar to those of camera shake caused by the human 
hand. Using advanced measurement technology, CIPA measured the vibration of a handheld camera, 
analyzed the roll vibration component, and successfully standardized the roll vibration waveform for 
the vibration table in this revision. This standard makes it possible to induce vibration and take 
measurements in a state closer to the camera shake caused by the human hand than the 2015 standard 
DC-001-2015 by using three-axis vibration including yaw and pitch, which are particularly dominant 
vibration components of the captured image, and roll, a component having the next largest impact.

 The first reason for measurement based on all three axes is to simulate camera shake in a state closer 
to that caused by the human hand. Therefore, regardless of the image height of measurement, vibration 
on the three axes of yaw, pitch, and roll shall be required.

Explanation of Shift Handheld Blur 
Current cameras are sometimes equipped with a mechanism configured to reduce handheld blur 
translating in two axial directions within a plane orthogonal to the optical axis. This handheld blur is 
referred to as "shift handheld blur.” 
The impact of shift handheld blur appears at imaging distances closer than about 20 times the 35mm 
film equivalent focal length, and is even greater at magnifications close to 1x (equal magnification) 
with lenses called macro lenses or micro lenses. 
 
Why Shift Image Stabilization Was Not Standardized in this Revision 
In order to properly evaluate shift image stabilization, the shift vibration waveform and imaging 
magnification shall be appropriately set. With the distance being so close, the measurement accuracy 
is greatly affected by the positioning accuracy of the camera on the vibration table, the positioning 
accuracy of the center of rotation of the vibration table, and the positioning accuracy and squareness 
of the chart. In addition, changes in focus during vibration also impact measurement accuracy. 
However, at this time, without an evaluation method properly satisfying these conditions, this 
standard does not incorporate a method for measuring shift image stabilization performance.

(CIPA DC-011–2015) and 2024 Standard (CIPA DC-X011-2024) 
 In the 2015 standard (CIPA DC-011-2015), yaw and pitch vibration waveforms were used to 
measure the number of stops of image stabilization performance at the center of the image at a 
handheld blur determination level of 63μm. 
In the 2024 standard (CIPA DC-X011-2024), the roll vibration waveform is newly added to the 
yaw and pitch vibration waveforms of the 2015 standard to measure the number of stops of image 
stabilization performance at the center of the image and at image height 60% at a handheld blur 
determination level of 20μm. 
 
 With the handheld blur determination level being tightened from 63μm to 20μm, the amount of 
handheld blur in an image shall be reduced on the camera to 20μm or less, possibly changing the 
number of stops of image stabilization performance measured with the camera. 
 
 Further, with a roll vibration waveform being added to the yaw and pitch vibration waveforms, the 
three vibration components shall be accurately separated into the yaw, pitch, and roll vibration 
components and detected on the camera. Thus, while the 2015 standard only required the separate 
detection of two components, the 2024 standard requires separate detection of three components, 
which increases technical difficulty and, as a result, is likely to reduce the number of stops of image 
stabilization performance.

Appendix (Informative) 
 
Explanation of Terminology 
Relating to Handheld Blur for Digital Cameras 
 
A-1 Introduction 
 
CIPA has issued the “CIPA DCG-002-2012 Specification Guideline for Digital Cameras (Revised 
Version)” as a guideline for describing the specifications of digital cameras in brochures and the like. 
The document includes definitions of terminology relating to handheld blur, and how it should be used. 
Such terminology includes “image stabilization” and “blur reduction,” and also “blur suppression,” 
which is a generic term encompassing the former two expressions. 
However, at present, it is possible to find examples in brochures and on the websites of suppliers 
where such expressions relating to handheld blur have been defined differently or without clear 
distinctions between them. Aside from causing confusion for users, such misuse of terminology 
prevents fair competition between rival enterprises. 
For the above reason, this Appendix seeks to systematically reorganize the terminology relating to 
handheld blur used for digital cameras and provides a definition-based explanation.  
The same information in this appendix also forms the content of the appendix of “CIPA DCG-007-
2015 Description method for Image Stabilization Performance of Digital Cameras (Electronic/Hybrid 
Systems).” 
 
A-2 Basic concept of this Appendix 
 
“CIPA DCG-002-2012 Specification Guideline for Digital Cameras (Revised Version)” contains 
“Commentary (6)-3. Further clarifications of ‘image stabilizer’ and ‘blur reduction’,” in which 
examples are provided of how “(a) image stabilizer” and “(b) blur reduction {mode}” as described in 
“Item 17: Blur Suppression” are respectively used. This appendix reproduces Commentary (6)-3 but 
provides more, detailed explanation. 
 
A-3 Explanation 
 
In line with the establishment of “CIPA DCG-002-2012 Specification Guideline for Digital Cameras 
(Revised Version),” “CIPA DCG-007-2015 Description Method for Image Stabilization Performance 
of Digital Cameras (Electronic/Hybrid Systems),” and this present standard, further clarification is

given of the definitions of “image stabilization” and “blur reduction.” Also indicated are “terminology 
relating to blurring,” examples of “output by blur detecting devices,”  definition of the “image 
stabilizer” function and examples, the definition of “blur reduction” and examples, cases where the 
image stabilizer function has a different method and subject, and when both the image stabilizer 
function and blur reduction are present. 
 
A-3-1 Terminology relating to blurring 
 
“Blur” is interpreted as displacement from a given position, and when used as camera terminology, 
blur can be broadly classified into “handheld blur” and “motion blur”. 
“Handheld blur” refers to blurring of the subject in shot images as a result of camera movement due 
to instability of the hand(s) holding the camera. “Motion blur” refers to blurring of the subject in shot 
images as a result of movement of part or all of the subject during shooting irrespective of whether or 
not there is handheld blur. 
Note that although “vibration isolation” is known as a method of preventing shake, “vibration 
isolation” in used in regard to equipment that prevents vibration from being applied to the camera 
itself, and is therefore terminology from a different field to image stabilization.

This refers to a function or mode that reduces blurring in output images caused by

speed as a result of optimization by an exposure control program. Although blur 
reduction is one method of an exposure control mode and a sensitivity control mode, there 
are no problems with this function being separately referred to as a blur reduction

A-3-3 Explanation of “image stabilizer” and examples 
 
A-3-3-1 Explanation 
The image stabilizer function is interpreted here to mean a function that uses the output by a blur 
DRAFT

76 
 
detection device to correct blurring in output images caused by movement of a camera itself (due to 
handheld blur or the like) and/or movement of the subject. Accordingly, if there is significant 
deterioration in image quality as a side effect of correction when no significant correcting effect has 
been achieved, or when the probability of a correcting effect being obtained is extremely low, it is not 
possible to regard the technology being used as “image stabilizer” technology. 
 
A-3-3-2 Optical, electronic, and hybrid systems 
Out of the examples of the image stabilizer function given below in A-3-3-3, a) and b) are called 
optical systems, while c) to e) are called electronic systems. Systems that use a combination of optical 
and electronic technology are called “hybrid systems” and usually have characteristics of both types 
of technology. Examples of hybrid systems are listed below. 
･ Systems where optical and electronic technology operates simultaneously. 
･ Systems that appropriately switch between optical and electronic technology according to the 
shooting conditions and the extent of the handheld blur. 
･ Systems where correction according to an optical system and correction according to an electronic 
system are carried out on different axes, with both systems being present. 
 
A-3-3-3 Examples of systems that are considered to be suitable for classification as “image 
stabilizer” systems. (NB. These are just examples, and the image stabilizer function is not limited 
to such.) 
 
a) A method where subject displacement in the image caused by handheld blur is corrected by 
moving part or all of the lens in parallel relative to the image sensor using the output by a blur 
detection device. 
b) A method where subject displacement in the image caused by handheld blur is corrected by 
rotating or moving in a perpendicular direction relative to the optical axis using the output by a 
blur detection device. 
c) A method where multiple images with reduced blur are taken at a fast shutter speed and such 
images are combined (aligned etc.) using the output by a blur detection device to obtain an 
output image with reduced blur and virtually the same image quality (S/N) as an image shot at 
the usual shutter speed. 
d) A method where an image with reduced blur shot at a fast shutter speed and an image shot at the 
usual shutter speed are combined (aligned etc.) using the output by a blur detection device to 
obtain an output image with reduced blur and virtually the same image quality (S/N) as an 
image shot at just the usual shutter speed. 
e) A method that is based on processing using a point spread function, an inverse filter or the like

77 
 
and uses information output by a blur detection device to restore an image with little blur from 
a shot image. 
 
 
A-3-4 Explanation of “blur reduction” and examples 
 
A-3-4-1 Explanation 
Blur reduction is interpreted here to mean a function or mode of reducing blurring in output images 
that is caused by movement of the subject and/or handheld blur. It is mainly achieved using a fast 
shutter speed as a result of optimization by an exposure control program. In this way, since “image 
stabilization” and “blur reduction” have very different technical aspects, such expressions should not 
be confused. 
 
A-3-4-2 Characteristics of “blur reduction” 
Example characteristics of blur reduction are given below. 
Merits: Since blur reduction can be achieved simply by optimization of an exposure control program, 
implementation is simple and such technology is also effective against motion blur. 
Demerits: Since high ISO sensitivity is required in many cases, there is increased noise in the shot 
images. 
 
A-3-4-3 Difference in image quality between “image stabilizer” and “blur reduction”  
Fig. 1 shows an example comparison between the blur suppression effects of the image stabilizer 
function and blur reduction. Although setting the functions of image stabilizer and blur reduction to 
ON results in a similar reduction in handheld blur, an increase in noise is observed in the image with 
blur reduction. 
In this way, since blur reduction is a method that suppresses handheld blur while mainly sacrificing 
image quality (and S/N in particular), it is appropriate to regard blur reduction as having no image 
stabilization effect. Accordingly, when measuring and describing image stabilization effects, it is 
necessary to pay particular attention to excluding effects achieved by blur reduction.

A-3-5 Objective axis-dependent and still- or video-dependent image stabilization 
 
In a camera equipped with a hybrid image stabilizer function, if such camera has several axes where 
correction is carried out optically and other axes where correction is carried out electronically, this 
should be clearly indicated to users (see a) below). If the  image stabilizer function is also enabled 
during the shooting of video images, when the correcting system or objective axes for correction are 
different to those used in shooting still images, care should be taken to clearly indicate such to users 
(see b) below). 
 
a) Yaw/pitch is optically corrected, while up-down, left-right, and rotation are electronically 
corrected. 
b) Correction is carried out on two axes (yaw and pitch) when shooting still images and correction 
is further carried out on the up-down, left-right, and rotational axes (i.e., a total of five axes) 
when shooting video images. 
 
A-3-6 Combined use of image stabilizer function and blur reduction 
 
As described earlier, since the effect of the image stabilizer function and the effect of blur reduction 
cannot be directly compared, when measuring the effect of the electronic image stabilizer function, it 
is necessary to completely eliminate the effect of blur reduction. However, since there are many 
cameras that exhibit the properties described in a) and b) below, sufficient attention needs to be paid 
when measuring and describing the effect of the image stabilizer function. 
 
a) A configuration that functions only as an image stabilizer when there is little blurring, but 
increasingly functions as a handheld blur reducer according to blurring level. It functions as 
only a handheld blur reducer at or above a given level of blurring. 
b) A configuration that functions as an image stabilizer and handheld blur reducer when there is 
little blurring, but functions as only a handheld blur reducer at or above a given level of blurring.
